Concept: what makes health data "spatial"?

Geocoded means every record carries a location — a latitude and longitude. That single addition unlocks three questions no non-spatial table can answer: where is disease burden concentrated, how far do people travel for care, and what environment surrounds a patient or facility.

Dataset typeWhat each row representsTypical location info
Health facility registerOne clinic/hospitalLat/lon of the facility
Patient/participant recordOne personLat/lon of residence at enrollment
Survey clusterA sampled village/enumeration areaCluster centroid lat/lon
Why it matters: once location is attached, a health dataset can be joined to any other spatial dataset — environmental grids, settlement maps, travel-time surfaces — which is the whole premise of this course.

Concept: the silent float-corruption bug

Facility IDs look like F001, F002… Excel "helpfully" strips the letter and converts them to plain integers on export. When pandas re-imports that file, it often infers the column as float64 — so 1 silently becomes 1.0.

F001 --[Excel export]--> 1 --[pd.read_csv, no dtype]--> 1.0 Now a merge on facility_id silently fails: 'F001' == 1.0 → False (no error raised — rows just vanish)
Why this is dangerous, not just annoying: there is no exception, no warning — the join just returns fewer rows than expected, and that's easy to miss in a notebook that "ran fine."
The fix: always declare ID column dtypes explicitly — pd.read_csv(..., dtype={'facility_id': str}).

Recap

  • Geocoded data = every record + a WGS84 lat/lon coordinate
  • Always check for missing coordinates before mapping
  • Always load ID columns as strings
  • A scatter plot is the fastest spatial sanity check you have
