Concept: the time-varying location problem

We want a pregnant woman's air quality, temperature and vegetation exposure for every day from conception to delivery. The problem: we don't know exactly where she was every day — we only have three snapshots, one per study visit. For some participants, those three locations differ.

conception ───────────────────────────────────────► delivery (~280 days) │ │ │ visit_1 visit_2 delivery visit Riverside Ironstone Kopje (day 0) (day ~180) (day ~280) Which village do we assign to day 90? Day 200? Day 270?
Why this matters beyond this course: this exact problem — sparse location snapshots, dense exposure windows — appears in almost every longitudinal environmental-health study, not just this synthetic cohort.
Notebook 04 — Exposure Timeline Generation2 / 4

Concept: the nearest-in-time midpoint rule

The rule we use: each day is assigned to whichever visit location is closest in time to that day. The midpoint between two visit dates is the cutover point.

visit_1 (day 0, Riverside) visit_2 (day 180, Ironstone) delivery (day 280, Kopje) │←───── midpoint ≈ day 90 ─────→│←──── midpoint ≈ day 230 ────→│ Riverside assigned Ironstone assigned Kopje assigned to days 0–89 to days 90–229 to days 230–280

Migrants (~30% of participants) show a visible step in their location trajectory — and that step produces a real, measurable difference in computed exposure between trimesters.

Scale note: ~60 participants × ~280 days ≈ 17,000 rows here — fine for pandas. A real study with thousands of participants over years would generate tens of millions of rows, which is why production pipelines need checkpointing and incremental processing.
