The Climate–Health Imperative

20M Low-birthweight babies born globally each year
75% Of neonatal deaths occur in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia
2.5°C Warming above pre-industrial by 2100 under mid-range scenario (SSP2-4.5)
  • SDG 3.2 target: end preventable neonatal deaths by 2030. Africa is off track.
  • Heat stress, drought, and air quality are modifiable exposure pathways — unlike genetics.
  • African researchers are best placed to answer African questions — but need the data tools.
"Satellite data offers a potential solution to Africa's monitoring gap — 40 years of consistent, planet-wide environmental records with no field logistics required."
Open with the scale of the problem. 20 million LBW births per year is an enormous number — and the burden is concentrated in exactly the regions where climate change is hitting hardest. The key message: we have a problem, we have potential data, and we need the analytical methods to connect them.
Section 1 — The Problem

The Zimbabwe Context

Key Statistics (DHS 2015)

  • National LBW rate: 8.4% — above the 7% global average
  • Neonatal mortality: 27 per 1,000 live births
  • Under-5 mortality: 50 per 1,000 — geographic variation of 2× between provinces
  • Skilled birth attendance: 80% nationally but only 65% in rural Matabeleland

The Geographic Story

  • Eastern Highlands (Manicaland): cooler, wetter, greener — lower LBW
  • Limpopo lowveld (Matabeleland South): hotter, drier, more food insecure — higher LBW
  • Same health system, different environments — natural experiment
Zimbabwe has remarkable geographic variation in environment within a single country — from the cool wet Eastern Highlands at 1,800 m altitude to the hot dry Limpopo Valley below 500 m. This variation, within a shared health system and survey instrument, makes it an excellent natural laboratory.
Section 1 — The Problem

What the Literature Tells Us

Heat stress and birth outcomes

  • T3 heat stress activates heat shock proteins, causes uterine vasoconstriction, reduces fetal blood supply → LBW and preterm birth (Ha et al. 2017, Environ. Health Perspect.)
  • Each 1°C increase in T3 temperature associated with ~100 g reduction in birth weight in high-burden settings (Davenport et al. 2020, Nature Climate Change)

Drought, food insecurity and NDVI

  • Drought reduces NDVI → reduced crop production → maternal malnutrition → intrauterine growth restriction (Grace et al. 2015, Geophys. Res. Lett.)
  • NDVI during T2 (when fetal growth accelerates) is a reasonable proxy for dietary availability in subsistence-agriculture communities
The challenge: most of this evidence comes from high-income countries or small cohort studies. We need scaled, satellite-based methods to test these pathways across Africa.
The biology is well-established in laboratory and small-cohort settings. What's missing is scalable evidence across sub-Saharan Africa. That's exactly what satellite-linked DHS analysis can provide.
Section 1 — The Problem

The Data Gap: Why We Use Satellites

The ground station problem

  • Sub-Saharan Africa has ~1 weather station per 26,000 km²
  • WMO minimum standard: 1 per 900 km²
  • Many stations have >40% missing data in recent decades
  • Disease burden is highest in exactly the areas with the least monitoring

Satellites close the gap

  • Complete spatial coverage — every pixel observed
  • Consistent measurement methodology across decades
  • No field logistics, no station siting bias
  • Multiple environmental dimensions: temperature, rainfall, vegetation, urbanisation
Key concept: Satellite data is not a perfect substitute for ground observations. It measures different quantities (land surface temperature ≠ air temperature) and has its own error structure. Understanding these differences is essential to interpreting results correctly.
Coverage comparison:
🌍 Ground: ~sparse points → requires interpolation across hundreds of km
🛰 Satellite: continuous raster, 250 m – 25 km resolution depending on sensor
⚡ Cost: Freely available (MODIS, CHIRPS, ERA5) via Google Earth Engine

What is Geocoded Health Data?

Notebook 01 — Understanding DHS survey data and its geographic structure
Section 2 — Geocoded Health Data

The DHS Programme

  • 90+ countries, standardised birth recode surveys since 1984
  • 5-year recall of all births from all women 15–49 in sampled households
  • ~20 households per cluster, 300–500 clusters per country survey
  • Publicly accessible: dhsprogram.com

What we get

  • Birth date — as CMC (Century Month Code, months since Dec 1899)
  • Birth weight — m19, in grams, if weighed at birth
  • Place of delivery — m15: home / facility / other
  • ANC visits — m14: number of antenatal care visits

What we do NOT get

  • Individual GPS coordinates
  • Exact birth date (only month and year)
  • Gestational age by ultrasound
  • Any blood or biological samples
CMC = (year − 1900) × 12 + month
Convert back: year = floor(CMC/12) + 1900, month = CMC mod 12
DHS is the workhorse of global health demography. It's the best-available source of nationally-representative birth outcome data for Africa. But it was designed for demographic analysis, not environmental epidemiology — so it has real limitations we must understand. CMC coding is used throughout the dataset; participants should be comfortable converting it.
Section 2 — Geocoded Health Data

Cluster-Level GPS Design: Privacy vs Precision

How DHS geocoding works

  • Each cluster has one GPS coordinate — the centroid of its enumeration area
  • All births in the cluster share this one location
  • Coordinates are deliberately displaced to protect respondent privacy

Displacement rules

SettingMaximum displacement
Urban0–2 km
Rural0–5 km
1% of rural clustersUp to 10 km
Why this matters for us: When we link a cluster GPS to a satellite pixel, we may be assigning the wrong environment. A cluster displaced 5 km north-west may cross from valley to plateau, changing elevation by 300 m and LST by 2°C.
This is not a data quality problem — it's a deliberate design decision. Our job as analysts is to quantify how much this uncertainty affects our exposure estimates and conclusions.
The displacement is a deliberate and appropriate privacy protection. In many African contexts, knowing someone's cluster could identify them to a hostile actor. As researchers we need to respect this design choice while being honest about its analytical consequences.

Section 3 — Satellite Environmental Data

Zimbabwe's Climate: Seasons That Determine Exposure

The seasonal cycle

  • Oct–Apr (Rainy season): warm, humid, green. NDVI peaks Jan–Feb. LST moderated by cloud cover. Most births conceived and completed during this period.
  • May–Aug (Cool dry season): temperatures drop sharply. Southern Africa's "winter." NDVI declines. Food from previous harvest still available.
  • Sep–Nov (Hot dry shoulder): temperatures rising fast, rains not yet arrived. Heat peaks without cloud moderating. Vegetation at annual minimum. This is when heat stress peaks.
Critical insight: A pregnancy that begins in January (during rainy season) will have its T3 (weeks 27–40) in October–November — the hot dry shoulder. Maximum fetal weight gain occurs at peak heat stress.
The data pattern: MODIS LST in Matabeleland South reaches 42–46°C (surface) in October, while Manicaland remains 25–30°C. The same satellite product captures this 15°C contrast — this is our exposure variation.
This seasonal-pregnancy interaction is the core analytical challenge in the workshop. We need to correctly identify which season each trimester falls in for each birth. We do this using the birth month CMC and working backwards 40 weeks.
Section 3 — Satellite Environmental Data

El Niño 2015: When Climate Anomalies Hit Pregnancies

ENSO and Southern Africa

  • ENSO = El Niño Southern Oscillation — irregular warming/cooling of Pacific Ocean surface
  • El Niño phase → below-normal rainfall, above-normal temperature in southern Africa (teleconnection via the ITCZ)
  • La Niña phase → above-normal rainfall, below-normal temperature

The 2015 El Niño

  • One of the three strongest events on record (alongside 1982–83 and 1997–98)
  • Zimbabwe rainfall deficit: ~20% below normal, 2015–16 season
  • Temperature anomaly: +0.5–1.0°C above climatological mean
  • Government declared national food emergency; 1.5M people food insecure
Why this matters for DHS 2015: The survey was conducted 2015–2016. Births recorded in the 5-year recall window (2010–2015) span both normal and El Niño years. We can compare outcomes before vs during the event.
Research angle: If heat + drought causes LBW, births conceived during the 2015 El Niño should have systematically different outcomes than births in 2012–2013 (La Niña, wetter, cooler). This is a natural experiment embedded in the survey data.
Section 3 — Satellite Environmental Data

Reading Satellite Data: Key Concepts

LST vs Air Temperature

  • LST (Land Surface Temperature) = radiometric surface temperature, measured from space
  • Air temperature = measured 2 m above ground (weather station)
  • Over dry bare land, LST can be 5–15°C higher than air temperature
  • Over dense vegetation, LST ≈ air temperature (transpiration cools the surface)
  • Urban concrete surfaces: LST can reach 55°C while air is 35°C

NDVI interpretation

  • NDVI = (NIR − Red) / (NIR + Red), range −1 to +1
  • NDVI < 0.1 → bare soil, rock, water
  • NDVI 0.1–0.3 → sparse vegetation, degraded land
  • NDVI 0.3–0.5 → moderate cover, typical dry season savanna
  • NDVI 0.5–0.8 → dense vegetation, forests, irrigated crops

CHIRPS: hybrid rainfall product

  • Merges satellite infrared estimates with rain gauge observations
  • Result: spatially complete at gauge accuracy
  • Works well in regions with some gauge coverage (Zimbabwe: ~150 stations)
Rule of thumb: When you see large areas of LST ≥ 40°C combined with NDVI < 0.2, that's the high-stress exposure zone for late-pregnancy women.
Participants often conflate LST and air temperature. Be explicit: we are using LST as a relative index of thermal stress, not an absolute measurement of air temperature experienced by the pregnant woman. This has implications for how we interpret effect sizes.

The Spatial Join Problem

DHS cluster GPS
→
Nearest satellite pixel
→
Monthly environmental value
→
Trimester exposure metric
→
Birth outcome regression

Why it sounds simple

  • Spatial join: take lat/lon, find nearest raster cell, extract value
  • Standard GIS operation — GeoPandas, GDAL, or SQL spatial functions
  • One cluster → one pixel → one exposure value per time step

Why it is not simple

  • The GPS is displaced → we may extract from the wrong pixel
  • A 5 km displacement can cross an environmental gradient (urban edge, altitude band, valley/ridge)
  • The bias is systematic not random — rural clusters displaced further, which are exactly those with more environmental variation
Worked example: Cluster A is truly in a valley at 400 m altitude (LST 38°C). Its displaced GPS sits on a ridge at 800 m altitude (LST 30°C). We assign this cluster LST = 30°C. All births in this cluster have an 8°C exposure measurement error. In regression, this attenuates the heat–LBW coefficient toward zero.
The spatial join is the critical link in the analysis chain. Any error here propagates through everything downstream. This is why we dedicate a full notebook to it and why we introduce the displacement simulation.
Section 4 — Spatial Joining & Uncertainty

Quantifying the Displacement Error

Monte Carlo displacement simulation

  • For each cluster, simulate 500 plausible true locations within the DHS displacement envelope
  • Extract environmental values at each simulated location
  • Calculate the variance in exposure values across simulations
  • Result: a distribution of plausible exposures per cluster, not a single point

Our estimates for Zimbabwe

VariableMean exposure error (rural)
LST (MODIS, 1 km)±0.8°C per 5 km displacement
Rainfall (CHIRPS, 5 km)±15 mm/month
NDVI (250 m)±0.04 NDVI units
Is 0.8°C large? Yes. The expected LST difference between the hottest and coolest province in Zimbabwe is ~8°C. An error of 0.8°C is 10% of that gradient — enough to substantially weaken a regression coefficient.
Is 15 mm/month large? Yes. Average December rainfall in lowveld Zimbabwe is ~80 mm. A 15 mm error is ~19% of the mean — comparable to the inter-annual anomaly we are trying to detect.

Approaches to Handle Displacement Uncertainty

Best practice: Report your primary analysis alongside a sensitivity analysis. If conclusions change materially under different displacement assumptions, say so — and say why.
Section 4 — Spatial Joining & Uncertainty

The Ecological Fallacy and Its Inverse

The ecological fallacy

  • An association found at aggregate level (province, district) does not necessarily hold at the individual level
  • Classic example: provinces with more doctors may have higher mortality — because sick people move to provinces with hospitals
  • Province-level NDVI vs LBW might be confounded by urbanisation: cities are both low-NDVI and have higher facility delivery (and hence more birth weights recorded)

The inverse: aggregation as a feature

  • Individual-level exposure error (displacement noise) is reduced by aggregation
  • Province-level mean LST is robust to 5 km displacement errors
  • Ecological associations can identify important public health patterns even if causal inference requires individual data
The same analytical question — "does heat increase LBW?" — requires different methods at different scales. Neither scale is "correct." Both provide evidence.
This is a genuinely hard methodological point. Participants often think ecological analysis is just a weaker version of individual analysis. In fact, when exposure error is large relative to individual variation (as with DHS displacement), ecological analysis can actually provide clearer signal.

Section 5 — Exposure Characterisation

What is an Exposure in Environmental Epidemiology?

An exposure is any environmental factor that has a plausible causal pathway to the outcome, is measured at the right time window, and varies enough across the study population to be detectable.

Three requirements — and three common failures

RequirementWhat "right" looks likeCommon failure
Biologically plausible pathway Heat → vasoconstriction → reduced uterine blood flow → LBW Choosing an exposure because it's available, not because it has a known mechanism
Right time window Heat exposure during T3 (weeks 27–40) — when fetal weight gain is fastest Using annual average temperature instead of trimester-specific exposure
Right spatial scale Cluster-level or district-level; enough variation across study area Using national averages (no variation) or 250 m pixel values (dominated by displacement error)
Emphasise: exposure definition is a scientific decision, not a data decision. We don't choose T3 mean LST because it was the easiest metric to compute — we choose it because T3 is when the physiological mechanism operates most strongly.

T1 — First Trimester

  • Heat: May impair implantation, raise risk of miscarriage, neural tube defects
  • Drought: Reduced early maternal nutrition

T2 — Second Trimester

  • Processes: Fetal growth, placental development, maternal blood volume expansion
  • Drought/NDVI: Maternal malnutrition → impaired fetal nutrient delivery
  • Heat: Moderate effects on placental function

T3 — Third Trimester ⭐

  • Processes: Fetal weight gain (~250 g/week at peak), brain development, lung maturation
  • Heat stress: Uterine vasoconstriction → reduced blood flow → impaired weight gain
  • Heat: Can trigger preterm labour (prostaglandin cascade)
  • Drought: Continued maternal malnutrition
We focus on T3 LST because that's when the primary causal mechanism (vascular stress reducing fetal growth) is strongest. But T2 drought/NDVI is also scientifically motivated.

Section 5 — Exposure Characterisation

From Satellite Pixel to Trimester Exposure

Daily LST pixels (1 km)
→
Monthly mean LST per cluster
→
3 months averaged for each trimester
→
T1, T2, T3 LST per birth

Why monthly, not daily?

  • Daily MODIS LST has frequent gaps due to cloud cover — sometimes entire months are missing in rainy season
  • 8-day composites (standard MODIS product) already bridge many gaps
  • Monthly means are reliable and capture the sustained exposure relevant to pregnancy biology

Why trimester, not annual?

  • Annual average masks the seasonal signal entirely
  • Seasonality IS the biological mechanism — the hot-dry shoulder matters, the cool rainy season matters
  • Trimester mean is a pragmatic compromise: enough temporal resolution to capture seasonal variation

Ecological Analysis: Province-Level NDVI vs LBW

Finding

  • Province-level mean NDVI (dry season, T2 of births) vs province LBW rate: r = −0.67, p = 0.034
  • Higher vegetation → lower LBW rate, as expected from the nutrition pathway
  • Manicaland (Eastern Highlands): NDVI ~0.45, LBW ~6.8%
  • Matabeleland South (lowveld): NDVI ~0.18, LBW ~10.2%
  • Harare: NDVI ~0.22 (urban), LBW ~9.5%

Interpretation

  • Association consistent with expected direction
  • Partial correlation (n=10 provinces) — effect of NDVI persists when controlling for urban/rural status
Caution — ecological fallacy: This province-level association does not mean that within Matabeleland South, women living near more vegetation have lower LBW. Individual-level association is a separate question.
Strength: Province-scale analysis is robust to the 5 km GPS displacement problem — displacement is negligible compared to inter-province distances of 100–300 km. This ecological signal is probably real.

Section 6 — Environmental Epidemiology

Explaining the Weak Environmental Signal: The Attenuation Stack

1 GPS Displacement → Exposure Error

5 km cluster displacement means exposure metric is measured for wrong location. Measurement error in the predictor attenuates regression coefficient toward zero (bias toward the null). Magnitude: ~20–30% attenuation for rural clusters.

2 30% Birth Weight Coverage → Selection Bias

LBW is only measured for ~30% of births (those weighed at facility). Facility births are disproportionately urban, educated, higher SES — the group with lowest environmental exposure. This compresses the exposure range in our analytical sample.

3 Mediation and Confounding by SES

Socioeconomic factors both mediate (drought → poverty → LBW) and confound (poor households in hot areas) the environment–LBW relationship. Adjusting for maternal education may partially adjust away the environmental effect.

4 CMC Birth Month → Trimester Timing Error

DHS records birth month not date, and conception date is not recorded. Our trimester assignment has ±2 week uncertainty. Timing exposure windows imprecisely adds further measurement noise.

Any one of these four sources alone would weaken the environmental signal. All four acting together fully explain why individual-level analysis yields non-significant results despite a real ecological association.
This is the most important interpretive slide. A null result in this analysis does NOT mean environment doesn't matter. It means the data, as designed for demographic purposes, lack the precision to detect environmental effects at the individual level. This is a common finding in DHS-linked environmental studies.
Section 6 — Environmental Epidemiology

How to Get Stronger Evidence

What DHS cannot provide

  • Individual GPS → need dedicated cohort studies
  • Birth weight for all deliveries → need facility-based surveillance
  • Gestational age by ultrasound → need antenatal care record linkage
  • Longitudinal follow-up → DHS is cross-sectional

Studies that provide stronger evidence

  • PRECISE Network (Mozambique, South Africa, Zimbabwe): prospective cohort, GPS at enrolment, ultrasound gestational age, objective birth weight — exactly what's needed
  • AMANHI cohort (Bangladesh, Pakistan, Tanzania): 220,000+ births, GPS, environmental linkage
  • Effect sizes found: ~100g birth weight reduction per 1°C increase in T3 mean temperature (AMANHI: Wells et al. 2022)
Design checklist for an environmental birth outcomes study:
  • Individual GPS at participant's home
  • Enrolment before 28 weeks (capture T1 and T2)
  • Objective birth weight for all deliveries
  • Ultrasound-confirmed gestational age
  • Multiple environmental measures (heat, rain, NDVI)
  • Socioeconomic covariates measured independently
DHS remains valuable for identifying geographic patterns and generating hypotheses. Prospective cohorts are needed for causal inference.
Section 6 — Environmental Epidemiology

The Public Health Interpretation: Small Effects, Large Numbers

20M LBW births globally per year
200,000 Fewer LBW births if prevalence drops just 1% (absolute)
~100g Birth weight deficit per 1°C increase in T3 heat (prospective cohort evidence)

Policy implications from the evidence

  • Heat early warning: Alert pregnant women and health facilities when LST forecasts exceed threshold in late pregnancy season
  • Nutrition supplementation: Target supplementation programs for the hot-dry shoulder (Sep–Nov in Zimbabwe) when food stocks are lowest
  • Facility delivery in hot seasons: Incentivise delivery at cooled facilities during peak heat months — reduces thermal stress during labour
  • Satellite monitoring for decision-making: Near-real-time NDVI and rainfall anomaly maps can trigger early supplementation programmes
Even if individual-level effect estimates are imprecise, the direction is consistent with known biology and ecological associations. At the population scale, even moderate heat-LBW effects translate to hundreds of thousands of preventable adverse outcomes. This is why the public health and policy community needs this research.

Section 7 — GeoAI

Capabilities and Limits of GeoAI

What GeoAI does well

  • Data retrieval and filtering (SQL queries on structured tables)
  • Descriptive statistics: means, counts, proportions, distributions
  • Standard statistical tests: t-tests, chi-square, correlation coefficients
  • Regression models when the analyst specifies the formula
  • Generating map visualisations from spatial data
  • Formatting results tables and labelling axes correctly

What GeoAI does poorly

  • Causal inference — it does not know whether a confounder should be in the model
  • Context-specific interpretation — it doesn't know Zimbabwe's agricultural calendar
  • Detecting when an association is spurious vs real
  • Knowing when the data are missing not at random (MNAR)
  • Ethical judgements about participant data
GeoAI produces plausible-looking output whether or not the question was correct. A wrong SQL query returns a credible-looking table with wrong numbers. The analyst is always responsible.
Section 7 — GeoAI

The Responsible Use Principle

A tool that generates code or SQL on your behalf requires you to read and understand what it generated before trusting the output. This is not optional — it is what separates a skilled researcher from a data consumer.

Practical checklist before accepting GeoAI output

  • Read the SQL / code — does the WHERE clause filter what you intended? Are NULLs handled correctly?
  • Check the row count — if you expected ~6,000 facility births and got 20,791, the filter failed
  • Sanity-check the result — does the LBW rate (~8%) match the known national figure? If you get 42%, something is wrong
  • Check the denominator — are you dividing by all births, or only weighed births? These give very different rates
  • Reproduce one number manually — run a simple COUNT(*) yourself and compare
The PRECISE Shmron assistant shows its SQL to the user. If you ever use a GeoAI tool that doesn't show its reasoning, that is a red flag for research use.
