### Tokwe-Mukosi Dam test (2026-08-25) — confirms catchment-size theory for JRC, revises it for GFPLAIN

Note: correct spelling is "Tokwe-Mukosi" (Tugwi is a common alt. spelling, incl. in our own Justify docx). Coordinates -21.0628°S, 30.3944°E; sits on the Tokwe River just below its confluence with the Mukosi River; 96.4 km² reservoir, largest inland dam in Zimbabwe. Bbox tested: `[30.25, -21.25, 30.55, -20.85]` (reservoir) and `[30.30, -21.45, 30.65, -21.10]` (downstream of dam wall).

- **JRC**: works well here — 7,356 valid RP100_depth pixels (vs 35 at Chimanimani), realistic 0.1–14.4m depths; RP10 also 6,708 valid pixels. Confirms JRC is usable at large-catchment/dam sites, just not Chimanimani's headwaters.
- **GFPLAIN**: still mostly fails — only 1,246/22,490 pixels (5.5%) classified at all over the reservoir (rest nodata), and 368/22,801 (1.6%) downstream of the dam wall — and *none* of those classified pixels were "floodplain" (all came back "not floodplain"). This revises the earlier hypothesis: GFPLAIN's failure isn't purely a small-catchment issue, since Tokwe-Mukosi has among the largest catchments in Zimbabwe and GFPLAIN still didn't find a floodplain there. Looks more like a Zimbabwe-terrain-wide limitation for this specific product (plateau/lowveld terrain not producing the developed valley floors its geomorphic method needs, or the true floodplain lies further downstream near the Runde/Save confluence, outside the tested window).
- **ERA5-Land Monthly Aggregated** (`ECMWF/ERA5_LAND/MONTHLY_AGGR`): genuinely useful as a **temporal screening tool** — can't discriminate spatially (11km pixels) but gives a real month-by-month "was this plausible" signal. 2025/26 season at Tokwe-Mukosi:

  | Month | Precip (mm) | Surface runoff (mm) | Subsurface runoff (mm) | Soil moisture (L1) |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | Oct 2025 | 13.3 | 0.0 | 0.0 | 0.144 |
  | Nov 2025 | 120.8 | 0.7 | 0.0 | 0.272 |
  | Dec 2025 | 207.1 | 16.0 | 2.3 | 0.302 |
  | **Jan 2026** | **281.2** | **35.0** | **7.7** | 0.366 |
  | Feb 2026 | 48.2 | 0.5 | 0.0 | 0.244 |
  | **Mar 2026** | 252.9 | 19.4 | 4.7 | **0.397 (season peak)** |
  | Apr 2026 | 55.0 | 0.6 | 0.1 | 0.304 |

  Reads as: Oct/Feb = implausible, Jan/Mar = plausible, **two candidate flood pulses, not one** — March has less rainfall than January but higher soil moisture (antecedent saturation effect), meaning risk doesn't track rainfall alone. Use: narrow a whole season down to specific weeks worth pulling daily CHIRPS for (the same way we isolated Idai's exact week), rather than guessing a date within "the 2025/26 season."

**Emerging strategy**: the two flood mechanisms don't just need different hazard *inputs*, they need different *tools*. Small-catchment/cyclone sites (Chimanimani-like): our own geometric crossing-detection + MERIT Hydro + CHIRPS pipeline is the only thing that resolves them — JRC/GFPLAIN both fail there regardless of tuning. Large-catchment/dam sites (Tokwe-Mukosi-like): JRC's actual simulated depth can be used directly as a physically-validated barrier layer, stronger evidence than our own proxy index. ERA5-Land monthly screening applies to either, as a first pass to find *when* before committing to fine-grained *where*.

### GFPLAIN algorithm, decoded (2026-08-25) — explains the nodata result precisely, and gives us a reimplementation path

`GFPLAIN-master.zip` (Downloads) contains the actual Nardi/Annis algorithm source (3 ArcPy scripts — won't run without ArcGIS, but the method itself is fully replicable in open-source Python). Precise mechanism:

1. Fill DEM → flow direction + flow accumulation.
2. "Stream network" = cells where flow accumulation exceeds a **threshold area (`bl_tresh`)** — a required input parameter to the tool, not a universal constant.
3. **HAND** (Height Above Nearest Drainage) = each cell's elevation minus its associated stream cell's elevation (propagated via a watershed operation). Confirms HAND as the real underlying method.
4. Drainage-area-scaled water level at each stream cell: `0.01 × (drainage_area_m²)^0.30 × 100` (Leopold hydraulic-geometry power law), propagated the same way.
5. Floodplain = cells where HAND ≤ that water level.

**Root cause of Chimanimani's 100% nodata, precisely, now confirmed by the published paper** (Nardi, F., Annis, A., Di Baldassarre, G., Vivoni, E.R. & Grimaldi, S. (2019). GFPLAIN250m, a global high-resolution dataset of Earth's floodplains. *Scientific Data* 6, 180309): the public 250m dataset is explicitly stated to only delineate "floodplain delineations of river basins with a contributing area (A) greater than **1,000 km²**." Our largest Chimanimani crossing has 262 km² — under a quarter of that floor. This is not an inferred/guessed parameter anymore, it's the paper's own stated minimum-basin-size criterion. Also confirms `b = 0.30` as the paper's own calibrated global constant (exact match to what we reimplemented), and that the paper's own validation method compared GFPLAIN against JRC's 200-year European flood hazard maps — the same validation logic we independently used comparing our self-computed floodplain against JRC at Tokwe-Mukosi.

**Reimplementation path (not yet built)**: we already have the two hardest preprocessing pieces from MERIT Hydro (`dir` = flow direction, `upa` = drainage area). WhiteboxTools has `elevation_above_stream()` for HAND directly. Would need: pick a `bl_tresh` appropriate to our catchments (~0.05–0.1 km², not whatever the global run used), apply the same power-law formula. Could give us a genuinely working, self-computed floodplain layer at 90m for small catchments the global product structurally cannot reach.

### Self-computed floodplain — built and working (2026-08-25)

`implement_gfplain_hand.py`. Implemented directly in numpy on MERIT Hydro (90m) — no ArcGIS or WhiteboxTools needed. Traces every cell downstream along MERIT Hydro's D8 flow-direction codes (standard ESRI power-of-two encoding) to its nearest stream cell, memoized so merging paths reuse already-resolved results.

- Stream network: `upa >= 0.05 km²` (channel-initiation threshold, humid mountainous terrain) → 22,443 of 106,392 cells (21.1%)
- Water level per stream cell: `0.01 × (drainage_area_m²)^0.30` meters (exact GFPLAIN v1.0 Leopold parameters, unit-converted from the original's centimeter-based formula)
- HAND: own elevation − nearest downstream stream cell's elevation
- Floodplain: `HAND <= water level of that stream cell`

**Result**: 105,378/106,392 cells resolved (98.9%; the rest hit the raster edge before reaching a stream). Floodplain = 21.4% of resolved area. At our 343 crossings: **343/343 classified at all** (vs GFPLAIN250m's 0/462), and **all 15 of the top flood-risk-ranked crossings independently fall inside the self-computed floodplain** — genuine convergent validation between two methods built from different inputs (one from rainfall+drainage-area ranking, one from pure terrain geometry). 118/343 crossings (34.4%) overall fall inside the floodplain.

Map: `chimanimani_selfcomputed_floodplain_map.png`. Output raster: `DEM/chimanimani_selfcomputed_floodplain.tif`. Per-crossing comparison: `Chimanimani_extract/chimanimani_crossings_with_selfcomputed_floodplain.csv`.

**Extended to Tokwe-Mukosi (2026-08-25)**: `implement_gfplain_hand_tokwe.py`, same method, larger grid (743×496 = 368,528 cells, ~3.5× Chimanimani). Runtime still trivial (well under a minute).

- Stream network: 76,527 cells (20.8%), floodplain 24.8% of resolved area (366,791/368,528 cells resolved).
- **All 111 tributary crossings classified** (100%; JRC managed 0), 52 fall inside the floodplain.
- **All 3 JRC-confirmed main-river crossings also land inside the self-computed floodplain** — genuine cross-validation against an independent hydrodynamic model, not just internal consistency. Where JRC has ground truth, our terrain-only method agrees with it.
- All top-15 tributary crossings by the rainfall×drainage-area risk index also fall inside the floodplain — same convergent pattern as Chimanimani.

Map: `tokwe_selfcomputed_floodplain_map.png`. Note: several of the highest-risk "tributary" crossings are tagged `waterway=river` with substantial catchments (up to 407 km²) yet JRC still returns nodata there — meaning JRC's coverage isn't a clean function of catchment size alone, it's whether that specific channel is included in JRC's modeled river network representation, which can miss real, sizeable tributaries.

### v2 rebuild (2026-08-25) — floodplain-driven scenario + relaxed driving model

`chimanimani_v2_scenario.py`. Two methodological changes, both user-driven:

1. **Flooded crossings now = self-computed floodplain membership**, replacing the event-specific risk-index percentile entirely. 118/343 Chimanimani crossings flood (up from 17 under the percentile rule) — a bigger, more geomorphically-grounded disruption scenario, not tied to one specific storm's rainfall.
2. **Driving model relaxed**: `path` removed from `NON_DRIVEABLE_TYPES` (only genuinely non-vehicle infrastructure — footway, steps, pedestrian, cycleway, bridleway — stays excluded). Rural tracks tagged `path` in OSM are frequently still motorbike/4x4-passable; excluding them entirely produced a misleading "31% has literally no vehicle access" map. Now `path` is driveable at a slow 8 km/h (rough/informal), not impassable.

**Result**: baseline driving reachability jumped from 69% to **100%** of the population — the old "walking-only" zones were an artifact of the exclusion rule, not a real absence of any vehicle route. New baseline driving time is higher (21.0 vs 13.7 min) since routes now realistically include slow rough-track segments instead of being marked unreachable. Flood impact: walking +13.0% (was +13.8%), driving +14.5% (was +2.8%), 834 people cut off entirely in both modes (up from 194/727 — same underlying road-network topology now shared by both modes, just different speeds, so cutoff pattern is identical across modes for the first time).

**Visualization also rebuilt**: 2×2 continuous travel-time maps (mode × scenario), replacing the earlier binary red/green "reachable vs not" map that was hard to interpret. `chimanimani_v2_accessibility_map.png`.

### Tokwe-Mukosi dam-spillage mechanism — built and run end-to-end (2026-08-25)

Not just tested — the full accessibility pipeline was actually run for the dam-spillage mechanism, same shape as Chimanimani. Bbox `[30.25, -21.45, 30.65, -20.85]` (spans reservoir + downstream reach). Scripts: `extract_tokwemukosi.py`, `fetch_jrc_tokwe.py`, `tokwe_hybrid_scenario.py`.

**Important refinement to the "JRC works at Tokwe-Mukosi" claim**: JRC only returned a valid value for **3 of 114 crossings** — the 3 that cross the *main* Tokwe/Runde river channel (depths 6.0–11.5m, genuinely catastrophic). The other 111 crossings are tributary streams, which JRC does not resolve, same small-catchment problem as Chimanimani, just one level down the drainage network. So JRC isn't a full substitute even here — it's a strong, physically-validated answer for the handful of main-channel crossings, and everything else still needs our own method.

**Hybrid approach used**: JRC RP10 depth (>0.3m = impassable) for the 3 main-channel crossings, combined with our MERIT Hydro `upa` × CHIRPS rainfall index (same method as Chimanimani) for the top 10 highest-risk tributary crossings, using the **March 2026** window — the month ERA5-Land screening flagged as the season's soil-moisture peak.

**Results** (85,545 people in bbox, 4 facilities, baseline driving-reachable 95.4% — much higher than Chimanimani's 69%, reflecting this lowveld terrain has proportionally far more driveable roads, 87% vs Chimanimani's 19%):

| | Walking | Driving |
|---|---|---|
| Baseline pop-weighted time | 286.5 min | 77.5 min |
| Flooded pop-weighted time | 322.1 min (+12.4%) | 87.9 min (**+13.4%**) |
| Population with increased time | 33,260 | 30,964 |
| Population cut off entirely | 1,895 | 1,738 |

Notably, driving impact here (+13.4%) is far larger than Chimanimani's Idai driving impact (+1.6%) — because the JRC-confirmed flooded crossings sit on actual driveable roads (tertiary, track) that a meaningful vehicle-dependent population relies on, unlike Chimanimani's scenario which mostly knocked out footpath crossings that barely touch the driving network.

### Comparison against the original inherited plan (2026-08-25)

Re-read `GIS analysis.docx` (`Placealertlabs\Cascade\Fw_ Flooding in Zim\`) and its Figure 1 flow diagram (embedded image, South Africa/eThekwini-oriented: SA DEM, OSM roads/rivers, 100-year flood-lines, StatsSA SAL/ward population, normal vs. disturbed scenario, E2SFCA) against what we've actually built, to know what will need addressing when the plan document itself gets revised:

**Matches, adapted for Zimbabwe data availability:** flow-accumulation-as-flood-proxy (their TerrSet RUNOFF module → our MERIT Hydro upstream drainage area, then taken further into a full HAND floodplain); roads + per-type travel speeds (exact match); population grid (their StatsSA SAL → our WorldPop, same role); their fixed-hectare "requiring culvert" threshold (para 10, 122) was an early seed of what became the HAND/Leopold floodplain, not adopted literally.

**Deliberate divergences:** their architecture is a continuous friction-surface/cost-distance raster (lets someone walk cross-country at 1km/h to the nearest road); ours is a road-network routing graph (Dijkstra) - we cannot represent "no road exists here, people still walk through the bush," a real capability gap not just a data one. Their proposed software (TerrSet/ArcGIS+Spatial Analyst) → our Python/GEE open-source stack - justified (no proprietary budget, and this is what let us decode and rebuild GFPLAIN ourselves when the packaged tool failed; the original document itself doubted TerrSet could do full hydrological modeling anyway).

**Real, unaddressed gaps identified** (in addition to E2SFCA capacity and facility closure, both now underway - see below):
- **Ward/administrative-level tabulation** - plan wants spreadsheet output by ward/SAL (para 30-32); we've stayed at the H3 hex grid, no aggregation to Zimbabwe's actual admin units (ZimStat wards/EAs).
- **Crisis-response / multi-criteria priority-ranking layer** - plan wants facilities ranked by population × vulnerability × accessibility × disruption, and scenarios re-run under specific interventions (temporary health points at schools/libraries, new fixed/mobile clinics, CHW placement). None of this exists yet; we stop at reporting the disruption, not modeling responses to it.

### Facility capacity for E2SFCA (2026-08-25)

The original inherited plan (`GIS analysis.docx`, Figure 1) specifies full E2SFCA (Luo & Qi 2009) with a facility-capacity/competition term, matching Watambwa et al. 2025 — our current accessibility metric is the simpler unweighted `A = Σ(Pᵢ·Tᵢ)/ΣPᵢ`, with no capacity term. Investigated closing this gap:

1. MoH `Healthcare_Facilities.csv` (1,674 facilities nationally) has the right schema (doctors, nurses, beds, catchment population) but is populated for only ~1-13% of facilities nationally - checked, not just assumed. Only 1 of our 10 pilot facilities (Chimanimani Hospital: 5 nurses, 54 beds, catchment 12,335) has real non-zero values; the rest, including Neshuro *District Hospital*, are flat zeros - a reporting gap, not a real zero.
2. Checked WHO's SARA reference manual (WHO/HIS/HSI/2014.5) - confirms our variable choices (doctors, nurses, beds, catchment population) match SARA's own service availability/readiness schema exactly, and gives international benchmark densities (25 beds/10,000 population, 23 core health workers/10,000 population) for interpretation. But it's the survey *instrument*, not a Zimbabwe results dataset.
3. Searched for an actual Zimbabwe SARA/Health Facility Assessment report: WHO's own SARA reports archive is down ("currently in development"), no MOHCC-published national report surfaced. No usable public dataset found.
4. Built `facility_capacity_estimate.py`: normalizes the CSV's ~90 raw `TYPEOFFACI` strings into 5 canonical service tiers (clinic, rural health centre, district/rural hospital, mission/private hospital, provincial), then for facilities with no reported capacity, imputes the median beds/workforce from other facilities of the *same tier* that do have real reported numbers (min n=5 to estimate; `provincial` tier has zero real samples nationally, correctly falls back to NaN rather than a fabricated number). Explicitly built to generalize to any facility anywhere in Zimbabwe, not just the two pilot sites, per direct instruction - this is a workflow, not a one-off analysis. Sanity check: the district/rural hospital tier's empirical median (55 beds, n=11) almost exactly matches Chimanimani Hospital's real reported value (54 beds), an independent cross-check the imputation wasn't given.
5. Verified against all 10 pilot facilities via spatial join to the national table (nearest point, all matched within 1.7km, most <100m) - F2/Chimanimani Hospital correctly picked up its real data rather than the tier median; all others got tier-median imputed estimates. Nationally, beds are imputed for 88.2% of facilities, workforce for 84.9% - stated honestly, not hidden.
6. **Wired into an actual E2SFCA calculation** (`e2sfca_scenario.py`, driving mode, both sites): continuous negative-exponential decay `W(t)=e^(-βt)`, β=0.05/min (the original plan's own example value) rather than Luo & Qi's original multi-zone step-function version (which needs several arbitrary zone cutoffs, not implemented - stated honestly as a simplification). Step 1 `R_j = S_j / Σᵢ(Pᵢ·W(Tᵢⱼ))` (competition-adjusted supply), Step 2 `Aᵢ = Σⱼ(Rⱼ·W(Tᵢⱼ))` (accessibility per hex), reported as population-weighted regional score, baseline vs. flooded, respecting facility closure.
   - **Chimanimani**: 0.0006 → 0.0005 (**-13.6%**). F1 closes (R_j → NaN); F4 and F5's competition ratio roughly doubles as some demand drops out of their catchment.
   - **Tokwe-Mukosi**: 0.0004 → 0.0004 (**+0.0%** net) - a genuinely important finding, not a bug (checked against the map before reporting): strong accessibility *loss* concentrated northeast near F1/Neshuro (the worst-cutoff zone, matching the catchment-shift map), offset by real accessibility *gain* near F2/F3/F4 as reduced competition (fewer people can now reach them) pushes their R_j up substantially (up to 2.7× for F4). These cancel in the population-weighted total. This is exactly the kind of effect a simple travel-time metric can't see, and exactly why the original plan specified E2SFCA over plain mean travel time - "the impact looks the same" and "the impact is redistributed, not zero" are different, both true, findings.
   - Outputs: `{site}_e2sfca.gpkg`, `{site}_e2sfca_facility_ratios.csv`, `{site}_e2sfca_summary.csv`, `{site}_e2sfca_map.png`.

1. **No crossing capacity data** — flood_risk_index is a relative ranking only, still not physically calibrated. **Threshold method upgraded (2026-08-25)**: replaced the arbitrary "top 20" fixed count with a **top-5% percentile rule**, which scales consistently across study areas of different crossing density (top-20 out of 343 Chimanimani crossings = 5.8%, but the same "20" at Tokwe-Mukosi's 114 would have been 17.5% — inconsistent). Cross-checked against `mapclassify.NaturalBreaks` (Jenks) on the Chimanimani distribution, which independently found a real gap in the data near 12 crossings (~3.5%) — close to the 5% rule, giving some convergent justification. Re-ran both scenarios with the new threshold (Chimanimani: 17 crossings, was 20; Tokwe tributaries: 6, was 10):
   - Chimanimani: walking +13.8% (unchanged), driving +2.8% (was +1.6%), cut-off 194/727 (essentially unchanged) — finding is robust to the exact threshold near this range.
   - Tokwe-Mukosi: walking +8.9% (was +12.4%), driving +5.1% (was +13.4%), population-with-increased-time dropped substantially (33,260→10,406 walking) — **but population cut off entirely stayed exactly the same (1,895/1,738)**, because that outcome is driven by the 3 JRC-confirmed main-river crossings, not the tributary count. The most uncertain part of the method (tributary risk index) matters least for the most severe outcome.
   Still not a physically calibrated pass/fail threshold — that still needs capacity data or ground-truth validation — but no longer an unscalable round number either.
2. **Ground-truth validation still thin** — one strong circumstantial match found (Nyahode No. 4 Bridge, see above), from general web search rather than the specific MSF/Munsaka sources originally flagged, which haven't been directly obtained. n=1 is not a validation set; the "4 major bridges" the literature mentions were washed away regionally are not yet all identified (two sources hit 403 Forbidden).
3. **Missing land-cover-dependent runoff coefficient (C in Q=C·i·A)** — currently implicit C=1 uniform. **Correction to the reasoning (2026-08-26)**: 11.1km ERA5-Land resolution is NOT actually a strong blocker here — land cover/soil type genuinely doesn't vary meter-by-meter, so a single regional scalar is a legitimate use of an 11km pixel (correcting an earlier, imprecise "too coarse" framing). The sharper reason it wasn't worth implementing yet: `flood_risk_index` is used only for *relative ranking* (top-15 cross-check) — multiplying every crossing by the same constant C doesn't change rank order at all, so it would have been mathematically inert for every result produced so far (the 15/15 finding would be unchanged). The real remaining issue is temporal, not spatial: `surface_runoff_sum` is a *monthly* aggregate, not event-specific (Idai's 13–19 March behavior vs. "March's typical month") — addressable by taking the ratio `surface_runoff_sum / total_precipitation` for the specific event month (both bands exist in ERA5-Land Daily Aggregated) as a real, physically grounded C for that period's land-surface/antecedent-moisture state, rather than a climatological average. Where this actually matters: not the current ranking (inert there), but converting `flood_risk_index` into a genuine estimated discharge (Q, real units) once real crossing-capacity data exists to compare it against — i.e., this gap and gap #1 (no capacity threshold) are the same missing piece, from two directions. Not yet implemented. **Update (2026-08-27): the "single regional scalar is legitimate" framing above was itself challenged and didn't hold up** — see the dedicated entry below ("Runoff coefficient C: per-crossing sampling, not a bbox-wide average"). C is now sampled per crossing (same pattern as MERIT/CHIRPS), not averaged across the site, and is consequently no longer inert for the ranking cross-check. The temporal (monthly-vs-event-window) issue described above was already fixed separately before this — `fetch_runoff_coefficient` uses the event's own start/end dates, not a calendar month.
4. **Dam-spillage mechanism** — full pipeline built and run at Tokwe-Mukosi (see above); still uses the same self-computed-floodplain/JRC crossing logic as the riverine case rather than a true release-footprint model. A proper observed-footprint approach (Sentinel-1/Copernicus EMS for the actual spillage extent) would be a more direct fit for this mechanism but hasn't been attempted.
5. **Landslide susceptibility** — NASA GLC ruled out; NASA's active successor COOLR untested.
6. **Scale beyond this one bbox** — untested at district/national level. GFPLAIN and CHIRPS resolution both plausibly improve at district scale (more terrain variety, bigger pixel count); JRC probably still marginal (district ≈ 3,000km² total, no sub-catchment individually clears JRC's own threshold).

### Correction: the "3,000km² JRC threshold" was misattributed (2026-08-25)

## Why the original workflow failed in places (2026-08-26)

Root-cause commentary on the inherited plan (`GIS analysis.docx`), written after enough of this project was built to actually see where and why it broke, not just that it did. Five distinct failure modes, not one:

**1. It was a geographic transplant, and the transplant is where it failed.**
The plan's data list — SA 30m DEM, StatsSA SAL/ward census, and critically, *100-year engineered flood-lines* — isn't generic GIS practice, it's specific to South Africa's mature municipal/provincial engineering infrastructure (eThekwini has commissioned hydraulic flood-line studies; Zimbabwe has no equivalent product for either mechanism studied here). This is the single biggest failure, and it's structural, not incidental: the plan assumed a *data environment*, and that environment doesn't cross the border. Every other failure below is downstream of this one.

**2. The "obvious" fallback — swap in a global product — has its own blind spot exactly where we needed it.**
When the SA-specific flood-lines don't exist, the natural instinct is "use the international equivalent instead." That was tried twice, and both failed for the *same underlying reason*: GFPLAIN250m has a hard >1,000km² basin floor; JRC's flood hazard model has a real coverage gap below roughly 3,000km² (not officially documented as such — found empirically, see the JRC-threshold correction entry above). Both are built and validated at a scale that assumes real river basins, not small rural Zimbabwean catchments (Chimanimani's largest is 262km², two orders of magnitude under GFPLAIN's floor). "Use the global version of the same idea" isn't a reliable substitute strategy once the actual study areas are this small — that's precisely why the project ended up reimplementing the underlying HAND/Leopold algorithm from the published method itself rather than trusting any pre-packaged product.

**3. Facility capacity data exists as a *schema*, not as usable *data*.**
Different in kind from the first two. The plan assumes "facility designed capacities" as a straightforward input. Zimbabwe's MoH facility inventory has exactly the right fields (doctors, nurses, beds, catchment population — matching WHO's own SARA schema almost field-for-field) — but only 1–13% of the 1,674 facilities nationally have anything but a zero in those fields. The data source isn't missing; it's present and functionally empty. That's a harder failure to spot in advance than "no such dataset exists," and it's why the capacity work had to build a tier-based imputation from what little real data does exist (see "Facility capacity for E2SFCA" above), rather than just reading the column.

**4. The architecture (friction-surface + COST/ALLOCATE, TerrSet) assumed a level of off-the-shelf tooling this project's actual requirements don't fit.**
The plan's core need turned out to be *scenario perturbation* — remove this specific crossing, close this specific facility, recompute — not just "compute a cost surface once." That kind of bespoke, repeatable perturbation is awkward in TerrSet/ArcGIS's COST module and genuinely awkward in OSRM too (bakes edge weights in at preprocessing time — see the routing-vs-graph discussion). It's not that the proposed software was wrong for GIS in general; it's that it wasn't built for "remove these 118 edges and recompute everything," which turned out to be the actual job. That pushed the whole project toward custom NetworkX code almost by necessity, independent of budget.

**5. Where the plan was actually right, and this matters as much as the failures.**
The *conceptual* architecture — normal vs. disturbed scenario, friction surface → travel time → catchment reassignment → population overlay → capacity-weighted accessibility (E2SFCA) — held up. Every one of those stages exists in what's been built, just with Zimbabwe-appropriate substitutes for each SA-specific input, and in several places (the HAND floodplain, the facility-closure logic, the tier-imputed capacity) the result ended up more rigorous than the plan's original one-line description implied, precisely because the missing data forced it. The plan wasn't wrong about *what* to compute — it was wrong about *what would be sitting there ready to compute it from*.

## Runoff coefficient C, and closing the "arbitrary cutoff" critique properly (2026-08-26)

Two related pieces of feedback, addressed together.

**C implemented, event-month, from ERA5-Land.** Earlier framing ("11.1km resolution too coarse for per-crossing use") was corrected in the previous session turn - land cover/soil doesn't vary meter-to-meter, so an 11km pixel is fine for a *regional* scalar. The real value of implementing it isn't discriminating between crossings within one site (a uniform C is mathematically inert for that - doesn't change rank order), it's **cross-site comparability**. That's now demonstrated with real numbers, not just argued:

| Site | C (runoff/precip, event month) | Terrain |
|---|---|---|
| Chimanimani | **0.533** | steep highland |
| Tokwe-Mukosi | **0.059** | lowveld |
| Beitbridge (test) | **0.052** | lowveld, border town |

A 9× difference between the highland and lowveld sites, and the two lowveld sites landing within 0.007 of each other despite being computed completely independently - both results are exactly what real terrain/land-cover differences should produce, and a strong indirect validation that the coefficient is measuring something real rather than noise.

Implementation (`fetch_runoff_coefficient`, `apply_runoff_coefficient` in `flood_accessibility_pipeline.py`): C = `surface_runoff_sum / total_precipitation_sum` (ERA5-Land Daily Aggregated, GEE) averaged over the site's own event window (not a full calendar month - kept as event-specific as CHIRPS itself), clipped to `[0,1]` since C is a physical fraction. Written as a **new** column, `flood_risk_index_c`, into `crossings_path` for all three sites - deliberately not overwriting the existing `flood_risk_index`/`tributary_risk_index` columns, since the deck and earlier log entries document that column's definition as implicit C=1; redefining it under the same name would silently invalidate those descriptions. Caching handled via a marker file (`{prefix}_flood_risk_index_c_applied.marker`) since the actual output is a column inside a larger file, not a standalone one.

One real ordering bug caught before it caused silent data loss: `compute_floodplain`'s crossing-sampling step rebuilds `crossings_flag_csv` FROM `crossings_path` on every run (even a floodplain-raster cache hit), so a column written only to the flag CSV would be silently wiped the next time that step ran. Fixed by always writing `flood_risk_index_c` into `crossings_path` (the gpkg base file) and running `apply_runoff_coefficient` before `compute_floodplain` in `run_phase_a`'s order, so the flag-CSV rebuild naturally carries it forward.

**Event-rainfall gate, closing the actual arbitrary-cutoff critique.** Direct question surfaced a real, previously-unstated gap: the flooded scenario currently flags a crossing as flooded purely by floodplain membership (static, geomorphic) - it never checks whether the event actually delivered rain to that catchment. Run the "flooded scenario" in a drought month and, as implemented before this fix, every floodplain crossing would still be flagged flooded. **This is distinct from the old percentile-cutoff critique** ("no evidence the 6th percentile wasn't flooded and the 5th was") - that one was already resolved when the scenario trigger moved from a top-N/percentile rank on `flood_risk_index` to floodplain membership; `flood_risk_index`'s top-15 is now only a validation cross-check, not the flood definition. This is a second, separate gap in the same area: floodplain membership alone is necessary but not sufficient for "flooded *this event*."

Fixed in `build_graphs()`: a crossing now counts as flooded only if BOTH (a) inside the self-computed floodplain, AND (b) its site's event-window rainfall at that point is `> 0` (using each site's actual legacy rainfall column name - `idai_rainfall_mm` for Chimanimani, `mar2026_rainfall_mm` for Tokwe, `event_rainfall_mm` for pipeline-built sites, mapped via a new `rainfall_col` config field, since the three sites predate a consistent naming convention). Deliberately a minimal gate (rainfall > 0, not a calibrated depth/discharge threshold) rather than inventing a precise cutoff with no evidence behind it either - a real physically calibrated threshold still needs crossing-capacity data (Open Gap #1). Verified at all three sites: **0 additional crossings excluded** at every site, because Idai, the Tokwe-Mukosi window, and the Beitbridge test window all had genuine widespread rainfall across their bboxes - the gate exists and is now correct, but doesn't change any current published number, since none of the three studied events were actually dry. It would matter (correctly) if this pipeline were ever run for a genuinely rain-free window.

Re-verified all three sites end-to-end after both changes: Chimanimani, Tokwe-Mukosi, and Beitbridge all reproduced their exact prior Phase B numbers (travel time, catchment shift, E2SFCA) - zero regression from either the C column addition or the new gate.

### The event-rainfall gate was mechanism-blind - fixed (2026-08-26)

Direct question caught a real bug in the gate above, same day it was built: "what if it did not receive rain but a dam collapsed?" Dam-spillage flooding (Tokwe-Mukosi) is **not** locally rainfall-driven - a crossing downstream of a spilling dam can flood from released reservoir water while it's bone dry at that exact spot. The rainfall gate as first written applied uniformly to every site, which means running it against a genuinely dry month at Tokwe-Mukosi would have wrongly zeroed out the entire dam-spillage scenario - backwards for that mechanism, since the flood's cause has nothing to do with local rain.

Fixed: added a `flood_mechanism` field to each site config (`"riverine"` for Chimanimani and Beitbridge, `"dam_spillage"` for Tokwe-Mukosi). The event-rainfall gate in `build_graphs()` now only applies when `flood_mechanism == "riverine"` - dam-spillage sites skip it entirely and fall back to floodplain-membership-only, which is correct for that mechanism: the baseline-vs-flooded split already represents "did the dam release happen," a scenario assumption, not something local CHIRPS/ERA5 rainfall could ever answer. Re-verified Tokwe-Mukosi after the fix: identical numbers to before (the gate's *output* didn't change, since Tokwe's illustrative window already had rain everywhere - but the *logic path* is now correct rather than coincidentally correct). Chimanimani and Beitbridge (`riverine`) are unaffected.

No equivalent fix exists yet for dam-spillage's actual missing dynamic trigger (i.e., "did the dam actually spill this time" isn't checked by anything - the flooded scenario is still purely "assume it spilled, apply floodplain membership"). That's the dam-spillage-mechanism analog of the riverine rainfall gate, and it's still open - see Open Gap #4 (dam-spillage still reuses riverine floodplain logic rather than a true release-footprint model).

Reuses the per-hex travel times already computed in `run_mean_travel_time()` and the nearest-facility assignment already computed in `run_catchment_shift()` (both now stored on `self` for this purpose) rather than re-running Dijkstra a third/fourth time. ## Crisis-response layer: priority ranking, underserved areas, intervention scenarios (2026-08-27)

Closes the last major gap named in the inherited plan (`GIS analysis.docx`): "Crisis response modelling" - a multi-criteria facility priority ranking, plus re-running scenarios under specific interventions. ### Vulnerability: RWI, validated locally against the user's own DHS 2015 data, not assumed

The plan's ranking needs a "socio-economic variables of catchment populations" (vulnerability) term. No per-hex SES data existed in the pipeline before this. Rather than picking one source on faith, two were combined and cross-checked:

- **DHS ground truth** (`extract_dhs_wealth_clusters.py`): the user's own local Zimbabwe DHS 2015 download (`Documents\DHS\DHS_downloads\ZW\`) - `ZWHR72FL.DTA` household recode (10,534 households, `hv271` wealth-index factor score, continuous) grouped by cluster (`hv001`) and joined to `ZWGE72FL.shp`'s 400 geolocated cluster points (GPS displaced per DHS privacy policy: rural up to 5km/1% up to 10km, urban up to 2km). All 400 clusters matched cleanly (0 dropped for missing GPS). Round 72 = 2015, the most recent Zimbabwe DHS available - earlier rounds (42/52/61) exist in the same download but weren't merged in, since DHS wealth-index construction isn't methodologically comparable across rounds.
- **A real sparsity problem, checked against the actual pilot bboxes, not assumed**: only 1 DHS cluster falls inside Chimanimani's bbox (nearest 5.3km from bbox center, next-nearest jumps to 29km) and 2 fall inside Tokwe-Mukosi's bbox (6.5km, next jumps to 30km). DHS alone would give one or two wealth values per site, not a spatially-varying surface.
- **RWI** (`fetch_relative_wealth_index.py`): Relative Wealth Index, GEE `projects/sat-io/open-datasets/facebook/relative_wealth_index`, 48,812 points nationally at 2.4km resolution - dense enough to vary within a site, but a satellite/connectivity-derived proxy nobody had checked against real Zimbabwe data before.
- **Local validation** (`validate_rwi_against_dhs.py`): nearest-RWI-point sampled at each of the 400 DHS clusters (median match distance 1.03km, well under RWI's 2.4km grid), correlated against DHS's `hv271`. Result: **Pearson r = 0.837 (p=3.3e-106), Spearman rho = 0.767** - a strong, clean, real correlation (see `rwi_vs_dhs_validation.png` for the scatter, self-verified by reading the rendered image before trusting the number). RWI won the source-selection decision (`Population/vulnerability_source_decision.json`) on this evidence, not asserted - `join_vulnerability_index()` reads that decision file rather than hardcoding the choice, and keeps both raw `rwi` and `dhs_mean_wealth` columns on `populated_hex` either way, not just the winning one.

### `run_priority_ranking()` - six criteria, all reused from earlier stages, no new Dijkstra runs

Per facility: catchment population (from `_assigned_baseline` × `populated_hex.population`), vulnerability (population-weighted mean over the facility's catchment hexes), low accessibility (population-weighted mean baseline travel time), capacity exceedance (`1 - normalize(R_j)`, R_j now stashed from `run_e2sfca()` - a small, purely-additive change, zero regression risk since `e2sfca_result` and every existing caller are untouched), degree of disruption (population-weighted mean travel-time increase over the catchment, with fully-cut-off hexes treated as a 100%/ceiling increase rather than excluded), and flood-risk exposure (facility's own `at_risk` flag OR its catchment containing a flooded crossing, via `gpd.sjoin`). Min-max normalized, equal-weighted sum by default (`PRIORITY_WEIGHTS`, overridable), ranked descending. Outputs `{prefix}_priority_ranking.csv` + `{prefix}_priority_ranking_map.png` (facility points sized/colored by score, self-verified by reading both rendered maps - clean, no overlaps).

Results: **Chimanimani** - F3 (Nyahode Rural Health Center) ranks #1, F1 (Chimanimani Village Health Center, the facility that actually closes under flooding) ranks #2 - near the top as expected given F1's flood exposure and disruption terms, but not #1, because F3 serves a substantially larger (12,348 vs 6,197) and more vulnerable catchment with equally severe capacity exceedance - a genuine multi-criteria result, not a bug (F1's known disruption is real and shows up as the maximum disruption score in the table). **Tokwe-Mukosi** - F2 (Maranda RHC) ranks #1 on worst baseline accessibility + highest capacity exceedance + highest disruption, ahead of F1/Neshuro (#3, largest catchment but comparatively better accessibility/capacity) - consistent with the already-documented E2SFCA finding that Tokwe-Mukosi's accessibility loss and gain are geographically redistributed, not uniform.

### E2SFCA multi-zone comparison - a real invariant, not a bug

Implemented Luo & Qi's (2009, *Health & Place* 15(4)) original multi-zone weights - (1.00, 0.42, 0.09) for 0-10/10-20/20-30min, hard cutoff beyond 30min (the paper's own erratum states the Gaussian derivation implies 0.03 for the third zone, but the paper's *applied* results used 0.09 - 0.09 is what's implemented, since that's what the published, cited method actually computed) - alongside the existing continuous decay, via a refactored `_e2sfca_compute(G, label, decay_fn)` shared by both.

First run produced bit-identical regional scores between continuous and multi-zone (0.0006109632301251136 baseline, both methods, to the last bit) - looked like a bug, wasn't. **E2SFCA's population-weighted regional score is a mathematical invariant of the method itself**: `Σ(P_i·A_i)` telescopes to `Σ(S_j)` (total system supply) for *any* decay function, because `R_j = S_j/demand_j` means `R_j·demand_j = S_j` exactly, regardless of what shape decay function produced that demand. The decay function only redistributes *where* accessibility lands spatially - it cannot change the region-wide population-weighted total. This means the planned "compare continuous vs. multi-zone at the regional level" was checking two numbers guaranteed to match by construction, not by coincidence.

Fixed by reporting what actually differs: population-weighted mean `|A_multizone - A_continuous|` per hex. Chimanimani: 0.00026 baseline / 0.00021 flooded. Tokwe-Mukosi: 0.00031 / 0.00031. Chimanimani District (see below): 0.00056 / 0.00041 - larger absolute spread at district scale, consistent with more facilities and more spatial variation to redistribute across.

## Rainfall-intensity flood gate, a symmetric facility-closure bug it exposed, and pipeline-native maps (2026-08-27)

### The "yes it rained, therefore this floodplain crossing flooded" gate was premature

Direct challenge: the existing gate (a crossing floods if it's in the self-computed floodplain AND event-total rainfall at that point is `> 0mm`) only ever asked *whether* it rained, not whether it rained hard enough - or onto already-saturated ground - to actually disrupt a crossing. `> 0mm` is an extremely low bar (confirmed earlier this session: every studied event had genuinely widespread rain, so the gate never excluded anything).

An external source's four proposed fixes were evaluated on their merits, not taken at face value:
1. **Regional weather benchmark (50mm/24h)** - real, implementable now (CHIRPS daily data already in hand).
2. **Return-period/IDF benchmark** - **checked and ruled out**: the suggested dataset (GSDR-IDF, Green et al. 2026, *Sci Data*) has **zero stations anywhere in Africa** (downloaded and searched its actual 4,289-station metadata; nearest station to any of our three sites is in India, ~5,770-6,150km away, against the paper's own 200km validity cutoff). Not "sparse coverage" - total absence for this region.
3. **Antecedent soil moisture proxy** - checked (queried Earth Engine directly): `ECMWF/ERA5_LAND/DAILY_AGGR`, already used for the runoff coefficient, does have `volumetric_soil_water_layer_1`. Not used for the final implementation anyway - it would need its own site-specific saturation-fraction cutoff with no basis to defend yet, whereas an antecedent-rainfall-total proxy (mm) keeps the same already-trusted CHIRPS source and units as the rest of the gate.
4. **GloFAS/Sentinel-1** - not fresh: GloFAS *is* what the existing JRC flood-hazard layer is built on, and that family of data already hit a real, documented small-catchment coverage gap (nodata below ~400km² drainage area - most crossings here are smaller). Sentinel-1 SAR overlay remains a legitimate, still-unbuilt lead, already logged for the dam-spillage gap specifically.

**Implemented (options 1+3, adapted)**: `fetch_chirps_thresholds()` (new) fetches a 2-band raster - the single heaviest CHIRPS day in the event window (`daily_max`), and CHIRPS total for the 3 days immediately *before* the event window (`antecedent_3day`, an antecedent-precipitation-index proxy for "was the ground already wet") - sampled per crossing exactly like every other per-crossing signal in this pipeline. New module constants `REGIONAL_FLOOD_THRESHOLD_MM=50`, `ANTECEDENT_TRIGGER_MM=25`, `ANTECEDENT_SATURATION_MM=40` (standard Southern-Africa flash-flood benchmarks, not locally calibrated - still bounded by Open Gap #1, no real crossing-capacity data to calibrate against). `build_graphs()`'s gate is now:

```
flooded = in_floodplain AND (daily_max > 50mm OR (daily_max > 25mm AND antecedent_3day > 40mm))
```

Riverine sites only - dam-spillage keeps floodplain-membership-only triggering, same reasoning as before (local rain doesn't tell you whether a dam released water).

**A real, pre-existing bug this surfaced while verifying the fix at Beitbridge**: after the crossing gate correctly zeroed out all 30 previously-flagged crossings there (0 edges removed - Beitbridge's Feb 2025 window is illustrative, never tied to a real documented flood, and evidently didn't clear a genuine flash-flood-intensity bar), the site's travel-time numbers didn't move *at all* (still the old 7.1→13.1min/+83.4%/1,997 cutoff). Root cause: `load_facilities()`'s at-risk check only ever tested floodplain membership - **no rainfall gate at all**, not even the old crude `>0mm` one. The exact same "floodplain membership alone means flooded" reasoning the crossing fix addressed, still present on the facility side. Fixed symmetrically: facility at-risk status now uses the same two-part gate, sampled at each facility's own point via the same `_sample_chirps_thresholds_at_points` helper, same riverine-only mechanism branch.

### Static maps folded into the pipeline itself

User request: maps should be "naturally part of the pipeline products," not separate scripts that silently go stale when the underlying logic changes (exactly what happened here - the rainfall-gate change left every existing map stale until caught). Five new methods added to `FloodAccessibilityPipeline`, all called from `run_all()`, matching the pattern `_plot_priority_ranking_map` already used:

- `run_roads_crossings_map()` - structural QA map (roads/waterways/crossings by tagging status), unaffected by the flood gate.
- `run_floodplain_map()` - the HAND/Leopold floodplain extent, purely geomorphic, also unaffected by the gate.
- `run_rainfall_map()` - CHIRPS event-total rainfall, the causal input layer, also unaffected (it's an input, not a gated output).

While rebuilding the last one, found `facility_catchment_shift.py` (the old standalone script it replaces) had never applied *any* rainfall gate at all - it duplicated its own graph-building logic independent of this pipeline entirely, and only had branches for `chimanimani`/`tokwe`, silently falling through to Tokwe's config for any other site string (would have mis-plotted `chimanimani_district` or `beitbridge` as Tokwe-Mukosi without erroring). Guarded with `if len(cutoff):` before plotting - a genuinely zero-disruption scenario is a real, valid result this map now has to be able to render, not an error case.

Every site now produces six PNGs per run (`{prefix}_roads_crossings_map.png`, `_floodplain_map.png`, `_rainfall_map.png`, `_accessibility_map.png`, `_catchment_shift_map.png`, `_priority_ranking_map.png`) plus the existing GPKG/CSV/JSON outputs, all generated from and guaranteed consistent with that same run's own numbers.

Next time `build_graphs()`/`run_mean_travel_time()` is touched, add it then.

## Map cartography fixes: title overlap, and a real catchment-map redesign (2026-08-28)

Direct feedback on the first version of the pipeline-native maps (above): the accessibility map's title overlapped the middle panel's own subtitle (checked on Tokwe specifically), and the catchment-shift map "doesn't make sense" - facility codes (F1, F2, ...) with no name key, no consistent color per facility, baseline/flooded not shown side by side, and nowhere obvious on the map where people who lost access actually are. ## The "×" was never actually being drawn (2026-08-28)

Direct question on the redesigned catchment-shift map: "on all maps it says × shows who lost access but are we using × or we are using dark hexagons?" Checked, and the honest answer is dark hexagons - the × was never real.

Root cause: `self.populated_hex`'s geometry column holds actual hexagon **polygons** (built from `h3.cell_to_boundary`), not points. geopandas silently ignores `marker=`/`markersize=` kwargs for polygon geometries - they only apply to point geometries. So `lost.plot(ax=ax, color=LOST_COLOR, markersize=30, marker="x", ...)` was never drawing × glyphs; it was drawing ordinary solid-fill hexagons the whole time, while the title and legend text next to it confidently said "marked ×". The same dead kwargs (`markersize=3, marker="x"`) were present in `run_accessibility_map()`'s cutoff-hex plotting too, though that map's own title text never made a marker claim, so it wasn't misleading there - just redundant.

**Fix**: removed the inert `marker=`/`markersize=` kwargs from both methods (solid hex fill is what's actually drawn, and is arguably better cartography here anyway - consistent with how every other territory on the map is already a filled hex, not a point marker). Updated every place the catchment-shift map's own text claimed "×" (panel title, legend line, docstring) to accurately say "solid black" instead, and added a proper color-swatch patch to the legend (matching the facility-key swatches) rather than leaving the black fill undocumented.

**A second bug the fix's first draft introduced immediately**: the corrected but longer panel title ("... lost access (shown in solid black)") collided with the "Facility key" heading in the corner - re-broke the exact class of layout bug fixed one entry above, from a different cause (text length this time, not missing `tight_layout` margin). Fixed by shortening the panel title back down and leaving the "what does solid black mean" explanation to the legend swatch alone, which already states it once, clearly - no need to repeat it in the title too.

Re-verified on Tokwe before re-running all four sites in full: legend swatch renders correctly, no title collision, and the map genuinely no longer claims a marker glyph it isn't drawing.

Cutoff counts staying exactly identical at Chimanimani and Tokwe-Mukosi isn't a coincidence to be suspicious of: "cut off" means a hex can't reach *any* open facility once flooded edges are removed, which is a pure road-network-connectivity question - it only changes if a new facility's road access specifically reopens (or an old one's specifically closes) one of those stranded pockets, which didn't happen at either site. The travel-time and E2SFCA numbers moved because more/different facilities change *which* facility is nearest and how loaded it is, independent of the cutoff question.

**Real bug this surfaced, not present at pilot scale**: the `chimanimani_district` re-run (42 facilities, the largest facility set tested so far) produced `NaN` priority scores for 2 of 42 facilities (F5, F13). Traced to `disruption_raw_pct` being literal `inf`, not `NaN` - `run_priority_ranking()`'s disruption term divides `(flooded_min - baseline_min) / baseline_min`, and a hex essentially co-located with its own facility has `baseline_min ~ 0`. For an *open* facility this self-cancels (`flooded_min` is also ~0 there, giving `0/0 = NaN`, already caught by `np.nan_to_num(..., nan=1.0)`). For a facility that *closes* under flooding but whose own hex is still reachable via some other facility (not fully cut off), `flooded_min` becomes a real positive number while `baseline_min ~ 0`, giving `+inf` - which `nan_to_num`'s default `posinf` substitution (numpy's built-in "replace with ~1.8e308") doesn't actually fix, it just delays: multiplying that near-max float by the hex's population overflows back to `+inf`, corrupting the whole facility's weighted-average `priority_score` to `NaN`. Never triggered at either pilot site (no closed facility's own hex happened to land in this specific "still reachable, but not instantly" case there) - a real edge case the larger, denser district run was the first to actually exercise.

**Fix**: treat a near-zero baseline (`< 1 min`) the same as a fully cut-off hex - the existing, already-documented 100%-ceiling cap ("a defensible cap, not a measured value"), rather than computing an undefined percentage. Re-ran `chimanimani_district`: F5 and F13 now rank 15 and 2 respectively with real scores (F13's disruption correctly hits the 100% ceiling), all 42 facilities valid, zero other numbers changed.

## Runoff coefficient C: per-crossing sampling, not a bbox-wide average (2026-08-27)

Direct challenge from the user on gap #3's stated reasoning: `fetch_runoff_coefficient()` computed one C value for an entire site via Earth Engine's `reduceRegion(mean)`, justified as "11.1km ERA5-Land resolution is fine for a regional C since land cover doesn't vary meter-to-meter." That argument answered the wrong question - MERIT Hydro (90m) and CHIRPS (~5.5km) were already being sampled **per crossing**, at whatever pixel each crossing's own coordinates fall in; nobody needed meter-scale precision, just the actual 11.1km pixel already available at each point instead of averaging across the ~6-30 ERA5-Land pixels a site's bbox typically spans. The single-value version was a genuine shortcut (avoiding a raster fetch, doing the reduction server-side in one Earth Engine call) dressed up as a resolution argument that didn't hold once compared directly to how MERIT/CHIRPS already work at comparable/finer resolution.

**Fix**: `fetch_runoff_coefficient()` now downloads a 2-band GeoTIFF (`surface_runoff_sum`, `total_precipitation_sum`, summed over the event window) at ERA5-Land's native 11,132m scale, cached to disk exactly like `fetch_merit_hydro`/`fetch_chirps`. New `_sample_c_at_points()` reads both bands and returns a real per-point `C = runoff/precip` (clipped to [0,1], falling back to 1.0 - the old implicit default - wherever a point has no precipitation or falls outside the raster). Both `rank_and_dedupe_crossings()` and `apply_runoff_coefficient()` now call this instead of using one scalar; a new `runoff_coefficient_c` column records the actual per-crossing value alongside `flood_risk_index_c`.

Because C is no longer a uniform multiplier, it stopped being mathematically inert for the top-5%-style ranking cross-check - it can now genuinely shift which crossings rank highest, unlike the old version which provably couldn't reorder anything. Confirmed real, non-trivial spatial variation once actually sampled per-point rather than averaged away:

| Site | Old (single bbox mean) | New (per-crossing range) |
|---|---|---|
| Chimanimani | one value | 0.470-0.547 (343 crossings) |
| Tokwe-Mukosi | one value | 0.040-0.103 (114 crossings, 2.6x spread - lowveld's more heterogeneous land cover than Chimanimani's highland pilot) |

`flood_risk_index`/`flood_risk_index_c` are validation-only columns - never read back anywhere else in the pipeline (checked directly: no other method references either column) - so this change cannot and did not affect any accessibility, travel-time, E2SFCA, or priority-ranking number at any site. Re-ran all four sites (`chimanimani`, `chimanimani_district`, `tokwe`, `beitbridge`) after this fix, combined with the facility regeneration and the disruption-formula fix above: every Phase B number reproduced exactly, confirming the change is confined to the column it's supposed to touch.

### Sentinel-1 observed flood extent, Idai / Chimanimani (2026-11-06) - first independent check on the HAND floodplain
Script: `fetch_sar_flood_idai.py` -> `SAR/chimanimani_idai_sar_flood_*.tif`, `SAR/chimanimani_idai_sar_vs_hand.json`. Adapted from the UN-SPIDER Recommended Practice (GEE JS), with fixes: dB *difference* not ratio; same relative orbit/pass for before and after; MERIT Hydro `hnd` < 15 m terrain mask instead of slope < 5 deg (would remove most of Chimanimani); JRC permanent-water mask and connected-pixel noise filter kept.
- Pairs: orbit 72 asc (12 Mar S1B -> 18 Mar S1A) and orbit 174 asc (13 -> 19 Mar). Post scenes are 2-4 days after the 15-16 Mar peak, so extents are **lower bounds**.
- **Radiometric shift**: median backscatter *rose* 1.4-2.1 dB scene-wide after Idai (wet soil/vegetation). A fixed -3 dB cut on the raw difference found almost nothing (68 cells at 90 m); the scene median shift is now removed before thresholding.
- Result (90 m cells with >=25% SAR water; HAND floodplain = 22,581 cells, ~21% of the domain): SAR flags 11-1,827 cells depending on pair/pol/threshold (largest: orbit 72 VH, -2 dB). Of those, 64-86% fall inside the HAND floodplain vs ~21% expected by chance. Only 0.0-5.5% of the HAND floodplain is SAR-flagged.
- **Reading**: where SAR sees new water it is mostly inside the HAND floodplain (supportive, modestly), but this is NOT a validation of floodplain extent: HAND is a broad potential-flood layer, SAR is a sparse lower bound (narrow steep valleys, layover/shadow, receded water, canopy). Result is highly sensitive to orbit pair (1,827 vs 162 cells) and threshold. `hnd` masking uses the same MERIT Hydro source as our HAND, so the overlap is not fully independent.

### Building exposure + monetary damage, Chimanimani (2026-11-06)
Scripts: `implement_gfplain_hand.py` (now also writes `DEM/chimanimani_selfcomputed_flood_depth.tif`), `building_flood_damage.py` -> `Buildings/chimanimani_building_damage.csv`, `..._summary.json`.
- **Exposure**: Google Open Buildings v3, confidence >= 0.7 -> 16,163 buildings (83.8 ha footprint, median 31 m2). 3,042 (18.8%) sit in the HAND floodplain; only 25 in the SAR-observed Idai extent (orbit 72, VH, -2 dB; a lower bound).
- **Depth proxy** = Leopold stage at the nearest stream cell minus HAND (floodplain median 0.39 m, max 3.44 m). A geomorphic scenario depth, not a simulated Idai depth.
- **Damage**: JRC global depth-damage functions (Huizinga et al. 2017, JRC105688), Africa residential curve (0.22 at 0.5 m, 0.38 at 1 m, 0.64 at 2 m, 0.82 at 3 m), Zimbabwe residential max damage 109.47 EUR/m2 structure, 54.74 EUR/m2 content (2010 EUR, not inflation-adjusted). Values read directly from the JRC spreadsheet.
- **Scenario damage if the whole HAND floodplain floods**: structure EUR 3.0M central (2.3M-3.7M using curve -/+1 SD); with contents EUR 4.5M (3.5M-5.5M). SAR-observed buildings only: ~EUR 75k structure (lower bound).
- Caveats: all buildings treated residential, footprint = floor area, JRC country-average replacement cost likely overstates informal rural stock; max-damage uncertainty not in the range; 90 m depth sampling.
- Known pre-existing issue: the crossing-sampling tail of `implement_gfplain_hand.py` raises `KeyError: ['status'] not in index` (after outputs are written).
