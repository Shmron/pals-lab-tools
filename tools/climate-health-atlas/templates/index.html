Data & methodology

This page explains where every number in the atlas comes from, how it was calculated, and how we checked it. Checks are described exactly as they were run. Where something has not been checked against an independent source, that is stated rather than implied.

How correctness was assessed

Three kinds of check were used, and each layer below reports which of them apply to it.

  • Completeness. Every district has a value for every year the layer covers, with no gaps, duplicate rows or missing days.
  • Plausibility and internal consistency. Values fall in physically sensible ranges, the spatial pattern matches Zimbabwe's known geography (for example, the hot Zambezi valley and lowveld versus the cool eastern highlands), and derived numbers behave the way their definition says they should.
  • Comparison with an outside reference. Only done where a published national figure exists (HIV prevalence against the headline DHS reports, district area against Zimbabwe's published area). No layer has been validated against local ground measurements such as weather stations, rain gauges or clinic records.

The climate, malaria and TB layers are published or modelled products used as released by their providers. Their accuracy is the provider's, not something this atlas has independently tested. See "What has not been validated" at the end.

Climate (heat & rain)

Method. For each district and each day, the atlas takes the mean of all ERA5-Land (temperature) or CHIRPS (rain) pixels inside the district polygon, using Google Earth Engine at each dataset's native resolution. ERA5-Land daily values are aggregated over UTC days. Every daily district value is stored, and all annual measures below are calculated from those daily values.

  • Mean daily max temp is the average of the daily highest temperature over all days of the year. It is not the average temperature, and it includes cool-season days, so it smooths over summer extremes. A year can look unremarkable on this measure while still containing very hot spells. Use the heat-day and heatwave layers, or the daily calendar in a district's Seasonality tab, to look at extremes.
  • Extreme heat day is a day whose maximum temperature is above that district's own 90th percentile of daily maximum temperature across 1990–2025. This follows the idea of the WMO/ETCCDI "TX90p" index, in simplified form: the standard index uses a separate percentile for each calendar day over a fixed reference period, whereas this atlas uses one percentile per district across the whole record. Because each district is judged against its own normal, a hot day means hot for that place: 35°C is extreme in Harare (usually low-to-mid 20s) but ordinary in Beitbridge. Each district's threshold is shown in its detail panel.
  • Heatwave days counts days in runs of 3 or more consecutive extreme heat days within one calendar year. Runs are not joined across 31 December and 1 January.
  • Heavy rain day is a day with at least 20 mm of rain (CHIRPS), the same fixed threshold everywhere. Total annual precipitation is the sum of daily CHIRPS rainfall.
  • Complete years only. A district-year enters the annual layers only if it has at least 360 daily values, so a part-finished year never shows up as a false dip. The current partial year appears only in the daily and typical-month views.

Checks run.

  • Completeness: 62 districts × 36 years = 2,232 district-years, none missing. Every district-year has 365 or 366 daily values. No daily temperature value is missing.
  • Rainfall gaps: the only missing daily rainfall values are 26 days at the end of the record (1–26 October 2026), where CHIRPS had not yet been released at the time of the last data pull. None fall inside a complete year.
  • Ranges: annual mean daily maximum runs from 22.3 to 32.3°C across district-years, and individual daily district maxima from 7.1 to 44.5°C. Annual rainfall runs from 150 to 1,696 mm.
  • Spatial pattern: the warmest districts by long-run mean daily maximum are Mbire (30.2°C), Gokwe North and Chiredzi, and the coolest are Mutasa (23.4°C), Marondera and Chikomba. The wettest are Mutasa (about 1,150 mm a year), Nyanga and Chimanimani, and the driest are Beitbridge (about 328 mm), Gwanda and Mwenezi. This matches the well-known geography of the Zambezi valley and lowveld, the highveld, the eastern highlands and the dry south. The area-weighted national mean is about 635 mm a year.
  • Definition check on heat days: because the threshold is each district's own 90th percentile over the same period being counted, every district should average close to 10% of days (about 36.5 a year) by construction. The measured long-run averages range from 36.8 to 37.2 days across all 62 districts, which confirms the threshold logic. It also means heat-day counts cannot be used to say which district is hotter. They show change over time within a district. Use mean daily max temp to compare districts.

Limits. ERA5-Land is a reanalysis and CHIRPS blends satellite estimates with station data. Neither is a direct station measurement, and this atlas has not compared them with Zimbabwe Meteorological Services stations or rain gauges. At these resolutions a large district gets one averaged value that can smooth over local extremes, and small or urban districts may be represented by only a few pixels.

Seasonality views (typical month and daily calendar)

The typical month chart averages each calendar month across 1990–2025: mean daily maximum temperature, total rainfall, and the number of rainy days (a day with more than 0.2 mm, which is a seasonality measure and is different from the 20 mm heavy-rain layer). A chosen year can be overlaid to show how it compared with normal. The daily calendar shows each day's temperature or rainfall for one month, with hot days shaded against that district's own extreme-heat threshold. Both views can include the current, unfinished year, because each month either has complete values or is not shown. They use the same daily values and checks as above.

HIV burden

Method. Prevalence comes from Zimbabwe DHS HIV test results (individual recode) for the 2005–06, 2010–11 and 2015 survey rounds, the only rounds with HIV testing currently loaded. Only clear results (negative or positive) are counted. Indeterminate and missing results are excluded, following DHS convention. Each test is linked to its survey cluster's GPS point, and the point is assigned to a district polygon. Prevalence is weighted by each person's DHS sample weight, because DHS samples some areas and groups more heavily than others, and a plain average would over- or under-represent them.

Checks run.

  • Completeness: all 62 districts have a value in each of the 3 rounds. People tested: 12,927 (2005–06), 13,791 (2010–11) and 32,002 (2015).
  • Comparison with published figures: rolling the district values up to a national figure (a mean weighted by number tested) gives 17.8%, 16.0% and 14.9% for the three rounds. The headline adult (15–49) prevalence published in the DHS reports is about 18%, 15% and 14%. The atlas figures are within roughly one percentage point of these, as expected. They are not identical because the published figures are rounded, cover a defined age group, and are computed directly from survey weights rather than rebuilt from district values. This confirms the national level only. It does not validate any individual district's value.

Limits. DHS is designed to give reliable estimates for the country and its provinces, not for each district. Districts have small samples: the median number tested per district is 146, 171 and 382 in the three rounds, and the smallest is 29 (two districts in 2005–06 have fewer than 50). Small samples mean wide uncertainty, and the atlas does not show confidence intervals, so differences between neighbouring districts should be read cautiously. DHS moves cluster GPS points by about 2 km (urban) to 5 km (rural, up to 10 km for 1% of rural clusters) to protect anonymity, so assignment near a boundary is approximate. Eligible people who did not agree to test are not corrected for beyond the DHS sample weights.

Malaria burden

Method. Zimbabwe's DHS rounds never ran a malaria testing module. This was checked directly against every DHS recode on file: the standard malaria rapid-test and microscopy columns exist but are empty in every round, and the only malaria fields with data are bednet counts, not infection results. Malaria values therefore come from the Malaria Atlas Project's modelled surfaces: Plasmodium falciparum parasite rate (PfPR2-10, the percentage of children aged 2–10 with detectable infection) and incidence (cases per 1,000 people per year). They are downloaded from MAP's public raster service at their native ~5 km resolution and averaged to each district as the mean of every pixel touching the district, the same way as the climate layers. The series starts in 2000 because that is the earliest year MAP models for this surface.

Checks run. All 62 districts have a value in each of the 26 years (1,612 district-years, none missing). District values range from 0.4% to 24.7% for parasite rate and from 11 to 391 cases per 1,000 per year for incidence, which are plausible for Zimbabwe. Limits. These are model estimates fitted by MAP to survey data elsewhere, not measured district counts. The atlas uses them as published and has not compared them with Zimbabwe health-facility malaria records.

TB burden

There is no district-level TB source for Zimbabwe. DHS never tests for TB (it needs sputum or GeneXpert testing, which is outside the DHS protocol). This was confirmed by searching the full DHS codebook across all 15 recode types and by scanning every DHS database on file for TB-related columns, with no TB data found. WHO's Global TB Programme publishes TB incidence (per 100,000 people per year) and the percentage of incident TB cases that are also HIV-positive at country level only. The TB layers are therefore national: they colour the whole country one shade per year, which correctly shows a national-only figure and is not a gap in the district data. Values are WHO's published estimates for 2000–2024 (25 years, none missing), used as released.

District boundaries

FAO GAUL 2015 level 2 gives 62 districts, grouped into 10 provinces using GAUL's own level 1 names (Harare province contains only two districts, Harare and Chitungwiza). The 62 district areas add up to about 391,700 km², within about 0.3% of Zimbabwe's published area of about 390,800 km². Boundaries are the 2015 version, so any later administrative changes are not reflected.

Province aggregation

Switching the boundary toggle to Province rolls the 62 districts up into the 10 provinces. Heat, rain, temperature, precipitation and malaria values are area-weighted means across a province's districts. Day counts, intensities and modelled rates cannot be summed meaningfully, and no population layer is loaded for these measures, so area is the only available weighting. A province with one small hot or high-burden district and one large mild one will therefore read closer to the mild one than a population weighting would. HIV test counts and positives are summed, and prevalence is a mean of each district's own DHS-weighted prevalence, weighted by number tested. TB is the same national value at every level. The 90th-percentile heat threshold belongs to each district, so a province panel shows the range across its districts instead of one number.

When a province is selected, each chart in its detail panel shows the province value as a bold line and every district in it as a thin line, so you can see whether the province average hides large differences between districts. Hover a point to see which district and year it is. The charts follow the year slider and autoplay.

Access to care under rainfall and flooding

Method. For each year from 2015 to 2025, travel time to the nearest health facility is calculated over a 100 m friction surface: roads from OpenStreetMap with speeds set by road class and surface, and off-road walking speed set by land cover and slope. Three versions of the surface are built: dry, rainy-day (slower roads, especially unpaved ones, and slower walking) and flood (rainy-day conditions plus road crossings inside the modelled floodplain cut). For each district and year, the share of days in each condition is estimated from daily CHIRPS rainfall and ERA5-Land runoff. A district has flood days when extreme multi-day rainfall (above that area's own 99th percentile) or extreme runoff covers at least a quarter of it; separate storms are counted separately, and two recession days follow each. Days with at least 10 mm of district-mean rain count as rainy days. The layers show the year's expected population-weighted travel time, which averages the three conditions by their share of days, and the percentage change from the dry baseline. Population is that year's WorldPop estimate. Province values are population-weighted.

Limits. Roads, health facilities and land cover are fixed at one recent snapshot, so year-to-year change reflects rainfall, flooding and population only, not new roads or facilities. The speed reductions for rain come from published studies elsewhere in Africa and are assumptions for Zimbabwe, not measurements. Rainy days contribute much more of the change than flood days do. The floodplain is modelled, not observed, and event detection does not catch every flood: rainfall alone missed Cyclone Idai (2019), and the runoff trigger is what picks it up. The event thresholds were tested at several values; the speed factors were not. Results have not been compared with observed travel times.

Composite risk score

The ranking view averages three measures, each rescaled to run from 0 to 1 across districts: mean daily max temperature and heavy-rain days (each averaged over all years) and HIV prevalence (averaged over the 3 survey rounds). The three are weighted equally. Equal weighting is a judgement, not something derived from data, and the score is a relative ranking of districts against each other, not a measure of absolute risk.

Climate × health correlation

This view pairs any climate variable with HIV prevalence, malaria prevalence or malaria incidence on one map. Each district is ranked into thirds (low, mid, high) on both variables separately, then coloured by both rankings on a 3×3 grid, so a glance shows whether the places doing worst on one measure are also doing worst on the other. It is a relative visual comparison, not a statistical correlation test. The thirds are recomputed from whichever districts and time point are on screen, and a visible pattern does not establish causation or statistical significance. The time axis follows the health variable: HIV uses its 3 survey rounds, and either malaria variable uses the annual 2000–2025 range.

3-year average and trend lines

On the charts, a thick line shows the 3-year average: each point is the mean of that year and the two before it (a trailing average, so the latest year is included and the first two years have no value). It is used to mask short-term year-to-year swings. The yearly values stay visible as a faint line underneath. The dashed trend line is an ordinary least-squares straight line fitted to every year in the series, and the figure shown with it is the slope converted to change per decade. It describes the overall direction across the full period. A straight line can hide changes that are not steady. Under each chart a one-sentence summary gives the rate of change, the highest year in the series, and where the latest year ranks. It calls a trend "clear" only when the slope is more than about two standard errors from zero (a simple 95% test that ignores the link between neighbouring years); otherwise it says there is no clear trend and that the change is within normal year-to-year variation. "Highest year since" refers to the start of that series, not to any longer record. Neither is applied to HIV, which has only 3 survey rounds. Both can be switched off with the buttons above the charts. On province charts the average and trend are drawn for the province line only. Temperature charts use an axis fitted to the data so that small year-to-year differences are visible.

Method notes on the map

The "How this is calculated" card at the bottom left of the map gives a short method note for whichever layer is selected, including how the climate × health view ranks places into thirds. It can be collapsed, and the full detail is on this page.

Ask AI assistant

The assistant turns a plain-language request into either a setting of this atlas's own map and correlation controls, or a chart built from this atlas's own tables. It never supplies numbers itself. Every field it proposes (layer, year or survey round, district or province name) is checked against the real values in the database before anything is applied, and a request that fails the check returns an error instead of a best guess. It is instructed to say it does not have something rather than approximate. It can still misread what a question means, so check that the controls it sets match what you asked for.

What has not been validated

  • ERA5-Land temperature and CHIRPS rainfall have not been compared with Zimbabwe weather stations or rain gauges.
  • No individual district HIV prevalence has been checked against an independent source. Only the national roll-up was compared with published DHS headlines.
  • Malaria Atlas Project values and WHO TB estimates are used as published, without local verification.
  • The access-to-care estimates (friction surface, rain and flood speed penalties, flood events) have not been compared with observed flooding or measured travel times.
  • HIV uncertainty (confidence intervals) is not calculated or displayed.
  • The composite risk score's equal weighting is not empirically calibrated.

Reproducibility and data currency

Each layer is built by a script that pulls from the source and writes to the atlas database. Heat, heatwave and heavy-rain thresholds are applied afterwards to the stored daily values, so they can be changed without downloading the data again. Climate data in the atlas runs to 26 October 2026 for temperature and 30 September 2026 for rainfall in the daily views, and to the end of 2025 in the annual layers, which only include complete years.
